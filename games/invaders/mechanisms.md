# Space Invaders — how the machine works

This map describes the parts of Space Invaders (Intel 8080, Midway `mw8080bw` board) whose behaviour has
been recovered into the idiomatic layer. It is a **current-state** description of the machine, not a history
of the port. Every claim is tagged with the confidence of its reading:

- **[seen]** — a role confirmed by a MAME observation.
- **[code]** — a role read from the routine bodies; MAME grounding still open. A stage-B grounding pass (boot +
  attract + a coin/start/1-player capture) confirmed a broad set as **[seen]**: the sound-port shadows and the
  fleet-march sound chain, the living-alien count and the alien-draw handshake, the alien-shot rate tables, the
  game-active and game-in-progress gates, the saucer launch timer, the shield save/restore, and the framebuffer
  draw/clear routines with their video-RAM constants. What stays **[code]** is what that capture could not
  exercise — the player-shot and scoring chain, the register-bridge delegators, and the still-disputed cells —
  pending a two-player / saucer / play-bot capture. The per-item tag lives in names.js (a cell's inline
  `// [seen]`/`// [code]`, a routine's `cert`).
- **[guess]** — an unclear reading; these keep a `loc_<addr>` placeholder rather than a descriptive name.

What is described here is the layer of **helper routines** — the primitives the game's spine calls to touch
video, sound, the alien field, the player shot and shields, the per-player records, scoring, and the frame
timers. The game's control-flow spine (the main loop, the object-state dispatcher and its handlers, the
interrupt handlers) is not yet recovered and is **not** described yet; a routine named `loc_<addr>` anywhere
below is one still awaiting a descriptive name — its mechanism may already be read at [code], its game-role or
naming still open.

## Video RAM and the framebuffer

The framebuffer occupies a fixed span of memory that begins at VIDEO_RAM_BASE (0x2400) and runs up to, but not including, VIDEO_RAM_END (0x4000) [seen]. clearScreen walks a pointer from the base, stores zero at every address, and stops once the pointer's high byte reaches 0x40, blanking the whole 0x2400-0x3fff window in a single pass [seen]. That window is 0x1c00 bytes, and the drawing code treats it as a grid of 32-byte columns: consecutive addresses step through one column, while adding 0x20 crosses into the neighbouring one, so the full width spans 0xe0 (224) columns of 0x20 bytes each [code]. Each byte packs eight pixels -- the coordinate mapping below divides a pixel position by eight to reach a byte, which is what fixes the eight-pixels-per-byte packing [code].

Two clears coexist because not all of the framebuffer is playfield. clearScreen erases everything, including the fixed heads-up margins [seen]. clearPlayfield instead starts at PLAYFIELD_VRAM_BASE (0x2402) and, as it walks each 32-byte column, steps over a six-byte margin: it writes bytes up through offset 0x1b, and once the low five bits of the pointer reach 0x1c it jumps forward six bytes -- landing on offset 0x02 of the next column -- and repeats until the high byte reaches 0x40 [seen]. The effect is to leave the two lowest bytes and four highest bytes of every column untouched -- the bottom status strip and the top score band -- while erasing the playfield between them [seen]. PLAYFIELD_VRAM_BASE is exactly VIDEO_RAM_BASE displaced by that two-byte bottom margin [seen].

At the foot of that preserved margin sits the ground line. drawBottomLine paints it by filling the lit byte 0x01 into offset 0x02 of all 0xe0 columns, one column-step (0x20) apart, starting at PLAYFIELD_VRAM_BASE -- a one-pixel line spanning the full screen width, laid down into the very region clearPlayfield refuses to touch [code].

drawBottomLine and the strip clears all funnel through a single fill primitive. fillScreenRow takes a byte value, a pass count, and a start address, then repeatedly stores the value and advances the pointer by 0x20, finally leaving that pointer one stride past the end [seen]. clearScreenStrip is its zero-fill front door: it forces the value to zero and drops into the same loop, blanking a run of columns from a given address [code]. Wider blanks compose from that strip. clearScreenRegion repeatedly clears 0x10-column strips, advancing 0x200 (one strip's worth) each pass, until the strip base's high byte reaches the terminator 0x35 -- used to wipe the reserve-life icon band before it is redrawn [code]. blankScreenStrip clears a fixed 0x20-column strip anchored at loc_391c, but only in one-player mode: it first reads the mode-guard cell TWO_PLAYER_GAME (0x20ce) and returns without disturbing the screen when it is nonzero [code]. loc_391c is a specific framebuffer address serving as that strip's origin; its precise role is not yet recovered, so it keeps its loc_ name [guess].

Sprites and other positioned graphics reach the framebuffer through a coordinate mapping. coordToScreenAddr takes a packed coordinate in HL, shifts it right by three -- dividing by eight to turn a pixel position into a byte position -- then forces the high byte into the 0x20-0x3f range so the result always lands inside the video window [seen]. The three low bits discarded by that shift are the sub-byte pixel offset, and they are not thrown away: seatBlitPosition captures them first, writing L's low three bits to output port 0x02 -- the bit-shifter's alignment offset -- before folding HL into a screen address through the same coordinate mapping [code]. Between them they seat a blit, with the shift offset choosing which of the eight pixels within a byte a sprite starts on and the byte address choosing the column; the sprite-drawing routines all position themselves this way [code].

Bulk moves in and out of the framebuffer rest on two copy routines. blockCopy is the general byte mover: it copies B bytes from the source pointer (DE) to the destination (HL) with both advancing, treating a count of zero as a full 256 [seen]. It is the workhorse behind record, template, and work-buffer copies as well as shield-buffer setup, and is not specific to video memory [seen]. captureScreenRect is the rectangular mover: for each of B rows it copies C contiguous bytes and then advances the source base by 0x20 to the next column, gathering a B-column by C-byte rectangle of the screen into a contiguous byte stream [code]. The shield save/restore drives it exactly that way -- capturing four stacked 0x16-by-0x02 blocks, and stepping DRAW_BLOCK_STRIDE (0x02e0) between successive block origins while a counterpart routine paints the stored rectangles back [code]. That stride works out to 0x17 columns, the spacing between the four shields [code].

## Sprite drawing

Space Invaders paints everything on the field -- aliens, shots, shields, score digits, the running text lines -- through one small library of column blitters, and they all share the same picture of the framebuffer: video memory is walked one screen row at a time by adding 0x20 to the destination pointer, so what the code calls a "column" is a vertical strip laid down the display. The routines fall into two families. One copies byte-aligned graphics straight into memory; the other pushes each source byte through the hardware bit shifter so a sprite can land at any pixel position rather than only on an eight-pixel boundary.

The plainest member is `drawSpriteColumn` (0x1439): over B rows it copies one source byte from DE into the screen at HL, bumps DE, and advances HL by 0x20 to the next row, returning HL sitting 0x20*B past where it began [seen]. A row count of zero wraps to a full 256-row pass [seen]. `drawSpriteColumn16` (0x1844) is just this with the count pinned to 0x10, preserving the caller's BC across the draw [code]. `drawSprite8x8` (0x08ff) is the glyph plotter built on top: it treats A as a sprite id, resolves its eight source bytes at an eight-byte-per-entry table based at `loc_1e00` [code] (the cell itself is still open, `loc_1e00` [guess]), writes A to port 0x06 [code], and hands an eight-row copy to `drawSpriteColumn` -- so a glyph is one byte wide by eight rows, and the call leaves HL advanced one glyph-cell down the screen [code].

That advance is what strings glyphs into text. `drawSpriteList` (0x08f3) walks C consecutive sprite ids starting at DE, drawing each through `drawSprite8x8` and letting the returned HL carry down to the next id, so a run renders as a line [code]; `drawThreeSprites` (0x08f1) is the same driver with the count seeded to three [code]. The game's fixed text lives on this path. Numbers ride it too: `drawDigit` (0x09c5) adds 0x1a to A to reach a glyph id and plots it via `drawSprite8x8` [code], and `drawBcdByte` (0x09b2) draws a byte as two glyphs, high nibble first and then low [code].

For graphics wider than a single byte that must merge over what is already on screen, `orBlitBitmap` (0x1a69) runs a two-dimensional OR-blit: an outer loop over B rows, an inner loop OR-ing C source bytes across into consecutive destination bytes, then the destination pointer reset to the row start plus 0x20 for the next row while the source runs straight through [seen]. This is how the shields are stamped down without wiping the background beneath them.

The shifted family exists because a sprite's horizontal position is a pixel coordinate, not a byte address. Each shifted blitter first seats itself through `seatBlitPosition` (0x1474), which sends L's low three bits to port 0x02 as the shift amount and then folds HL into a screen address via `coordToScreenAddr` (0x1a47) -- shifting the coordinate right by three (eight pixels per byte) and forcing the high byte into the 0x2000-0x3fff video window [seen]. With the offset seated, a source byte is fed to the shifter by writing it to port 0x04 and reading the shifted result back from port 0x03; writing a byte and then a zero yields the two overlapping halves a shifted sprite occupies, which land in two adjacent screen bytes (HL and HL+1) before the pointer steps down 0x20 to the next row [code].

The four shifted blitters differ only in how they combine those halves with the screen. `blitShiftedSprite` (0x15d3) overwrites, storing both halves straight in [code]. `orBlitShiftedSprite` (0x1400) ORs each half into what is there, preserving the background [code]. `eraseShiftedSprite` (0x1452) does the inverse -- it complements the shifted byte and ANDs it into the screen, clearing exactly the bits the sprite would have set [code]. `drawSpriteWithCollision` (0x1491) is the OR blit with a test woven in: it clears `COLLISION_FLAG` (0x2061) to zero at the start [code], and on each half ANDs the incoming shifted bits against the screen before OR-ing them in, so any overlap (a nonzero AND on either half) latches `COLLISION_FLAG` to 1 [code]. That is the hit test the game reads after drawing a shot -- `drawAlienShotWithCollision` seats the alien-shot descriptor and drops straight into it. `clearSpriteColumn` (0x1424) reuses the same seat step but simply zeros the two-byte-wide footprint over B rows [code], used to wipe a sprite when a prize despawns.

Two feeders drive the blitters from data streams. `fetchNextDrawRecord` (0x1856) reads a four-byte record through the BC cursor: a leading 0xff is the terminator and returns with carry set and BC left parked; otherwise it unpacks two little-endian words -- a screen address into HL and a graphics pointer into DE -- steps BC past all four bytes, and clears carry [code]. A draw-sequence walker pairs this with `drawSpriteColumn16`, pulling record after record and blitting each as a sixteen-row column until the terminator flags carry and stops the loop.

`stepAnimationFrame` (0x1868) advances one frame of a scripted animation held in a small block of state. It bumps a frame counter at `loc_20c2` [code] (the cell itself is still open, `loc_20c2` [guess]), folds the step at `ANIM_COORD_STEP_LO` (0x20c3) into the animation's two running coordinate totals -- one of which is `ANIM_SPRITE_COORD` (0x20c5) -- and compares the resulting total against `ANIM_END_COORD` (0x20ca); on a match it latches `ANIM_DONE_FLAG` (0x20cb) to 1 and stops [code]. Otherwise it takes the frame's sprite source from the base pointer `ANIM_BASE_SPRITE_SRC` (0x20cc), adding 0x30 to it while bit 2 of the counter is clear so the sprite alternates between two frames every four steps as it moves [code], stores that into `ANIM_SPRITE_SRC` (0x20c7) [code], loads the five-byte descriptor at `ANIM_SPRITE_COORD` (0x20c5) to recover the current coordinate and the source pointer, and shift-blits the frame with `blitShiftedSprite` [code].

## Sound

Space Invaders talks to two of the board's output ports. Port 3 carries the discrete one-shot cues (a player shot, a scoring cue, and the saucer's continuous whine), while port 5 carries the fleet-march "footsteps" and the saucer-hit tone. Because several independent effects share each port, the game never composes a port byte from scratch: it keeps a RAM shadow of each latch — SOUND_PORT3_SHADOW at 0x2094 [seen] and SOUND_PORT5_SHADOW at 0x2098 [seen] — edits individual bits in the shadow, and mirrors the whole byte out. Every effect therefore reduces to "raise this bit" or "clear this bit," with the shadow remembering what the rest of the mixer is doing.

Two tiny primitives do all the port-3 bit work. `startSound` (0x18fa) [seen] ORs a caller-supplied mask into SOUND_PORT3_SHADOW, stores it back, and writes it to port 3, turning a cue on; `loc_19dc` [seen] is the mirror image, ANDing the shadow with a mask to turn a cue off, and likewise writes port 3. Both leave the resulting byte in A. The callers choose the bit: a companion step keyed on the player-shot state raises bit 1 (mask 0x02) while a shot is in flight and clears it (mask 0xfd) when it ends [code]; a scoring path raises bit 3 (mask 0x08) as it stamps a score-add, gated on the game being in progress [code]; and the saucer logic drives bit 0.

The fleet march is produced by two routines working in tandem — a metronome on the interrupt and a bookkeeper on the frame loop. `stepFleetMarchSound` (0x1740) [seen] is the metronome, driven from the interrupt handler at 0x0010 only while GAME_IN_PROGRESS (0x20ef) [seen] is set. Each tick it counts down FLEET_SOUND_OFF_TIMER (0x209b) [seen]; when that reaches zero it runs the note-off helper `loc_176d` [code], which re-emits only the two high bits of the port-5 shadow (mask 0x30) and so silences the four low march tones — this is what makes each footstep a discrete note rather than a drone. It then reads an enable flag at 0x2068 (`loc_2068`, open [guess]); if that is clear it silences through `loc_176d` and stops. Otherwise it counts down the beat timer FLEET_SOUND_TIMER (0x2096) [seen] and returns until it expires. On the beat it writes the full port-5 shadow out (sounding the current tone) and — provided ALIEN_COUNT (0x2082) [seen] is nonzero — reloads the beat timer from FLEET_SOUND_PERIOD (0x2097) [code], sets the trigger FLEET_SOUND_STEP (0x2095) [seen] to 1, and arms FLEET_SOUND_OFF_TIMER to 4, so the note rings for four ticks before the off-timer cuts it. When the last alien is gone the beat plays but nothing is re-armed, and the march goes quiet.

`advanceFleetMarchSound` (0x1775) [seen] runs each frame from the main loop and does the pitch-and-tempo work the metronome requested. When FLEET_SOUND_STEP is set, it first chooses the tempo for how thin the wave has become: it walks the threshold table FLEET_RATE_THRESHOLDS (0x1a11) [seen] against ALIEN_COUNT, advancing a parallel index until the alien count is at least the threshold, and copies the matching byte from FLEET_RATE_TABLE (0x1a21) [seen] into FLEET_SOUND_PERIOD — so as aliens die the period shrinks and the march accelerates. Then it steps the tone: it isolates the low nibble of the port-5 shadow and rotates it left one bit, cycling the single lit bit 0x01 -> 0x02 -> 0x04 -> 0x08 and wrapping 0x10 back to 0x01, which is the familiar four-note descending loop, while OR-ing the two high bits (mask 0x30) back untouched. Finally it clears FLEET_SOUND_STEP. On every frame, triggered or not, it also counts down SFX_OFF_TIMER (0x2099) [seen], and when that expires it clears port-3 bit 4 (mask 0xef through `loc_19dc`) — a one-shot port-3 effect that auto-silences at the end of its window.

Alongside these sits `latchSoundPort5` (0x1770) [code], a bare helper that masks A to the two high sound-select bits (mask 0x30) and writes port 5 — used when code wants to emit those latched high bits directly; `loc_176d` [code] is the same tail but reads its byte from the port-5 shadow first.

The saucer's continuous tone is a single port-3 bit governed frame by frame by `updateSaucerSound` (0x1804) [code] off a two-flag gate. If SAUCER_ACTIVE (0x2084) [code] is clear it delegates to `stopSaucerSound` (0x0707) [code], which clears bit 0 (mask 0xfe through `loc_19dc`) and kills the whine. If the saucer is active but SAUCER_EXITING (0x2085) [code] is set — it has reached the edge — the routine simply returns and leaves the latch as it stands. Only when the saucer is active and not exiting does it call `startSound` with mask 0x01, holding bit 0 on. When the saucer actually appears is scheduled elsewhere: SAUCER_TIMER (0x2091) [seen] is a rolling 16-bit countdown — not itself a sound cell — whose expiry arms the next saucer, and the audio simply follows the resulting active/exiting flags.

When the player shoots the saucer down, `playSaucerHitSoundAndDrawSprite` (0x074b) [code] fires the hit. It ORs bit 4 (mask 0x10) into the port-5 shadow and latches it through `latchSoundPort5`, sounding the UFO-explosion tone; it then repoints the saucer's sprite record (0x2087) at the explosion-graphic table SAUCER_HIT_SPRITE (0x1d7c) [code] and draws that column, so the bang and the burst arrive together. That the hit tone lives in port-5 bit 4 — inside the 0x30 mask the fleet-march routines deliberately preserve — is precisely why the march can keep rotating its low nibble without ever clobbering a saucer-hit that is still ringing.

## The alien field and its march

Each player owns an entire 256-byte page of work RAM for its aliens. Player one's page begins at ALIEN_FIELD_P1 (0x2100) and player two's at ALIEN_FIELD_P2 (0x2200) [code]; the low 0x37 (55) bytes of each page form the liveness grid — one byte per alien, laid out as five rows of eleven — and a byte reads nonzero for as long as that alien is still on the board. Which of the two pages is currently in play is named by a single page-number byte, ACTIVE_PLAYER_PAGE (0x2067) [code]: shifting that byte left by eight yields the base of the active grid, so the same code walks whichever player's field is live simply by reading the page byte and forming page<<8.

A fresh wave is armed by markAllAliensAlive [seen], which writes 0x01 into 0x37 consecutive bytes from a caller-supplied address — fifty-five live aliens in one tight fill. Two thin seaters choose the target: markAllAliensAliveP1 [code] points the fill at 0x2100 and markAllAliensAliveP2 [code] at 0x2200. Notice these two name the page bases as fixed constants rather than going through the active-page byte, so each resets a specific player's grid regardless of who is currently active.

Counting the survivors is the job of countLiveAliens [seen]. It forms the active grid base from ACTIVE_PLAYER_PAGE, scans all 0x37 cells, counts the nonzero ones, and publishes the running total to ALIEN_COUNT (0x2082) [seen]. That published count is what the rest of the machine reads to decide how fast the fleet should feel — the fleet-march sound, for one, steps its tempo from it [seen]. As a special case, when exactly one alien remains countLiveAliens raises LAST_ALIEN_FLAG (0x206b) [code] to single out the lone survivor (its reader is not yet recovered).

Where the whole fleet sits on screen is tracked not per-alien but by a single reference corner, from which every alien's position is derived. loadReferenceAlienState [seen] rebuilds that reference from the active player's saved record, which lives at the top of the same per-player page. activeFieldRecordPointer forms page<<8 | 0xfc [code]; loadReferenceAlienState reads the 16-bit coordinate word stored there and mirrors it into two working spots — the pair at loc_2009/loc_200a and ALIEN_DRAW_ADDR (0x200b) [seen] (loc_2009/loc_200a themselves are still open [guess]). It then steps one byte below the pointer, to page-offset 0xfb — the per-player saved delta held at loc_21fb for player one and loc_22fb for player two [guess] — copies that byte down into a working count at loc_2008 (decrementing it by one when it reads 0x03) [guess], and when the byte carries the sentinel value 0xfe it raises FLEET_MOVE_DIR (0x200d) [seen]. Those delta bytes start life seeded to 0x02 for both players [guess]. Because the reference word and the delta are saved per page, each player's fleet keeps its own position and heading across the hand-off between turns; the save side stages the working count and coordinate word back toward the record [code].

The reference corner becomes an actual alien position through alienIndexToScreenCoords [code]. Given an alien's index (0..54) in L, it seeds two registers from the reference pair — one from loc_2009, one from loc_200a — then decomposes the index over 0x0b: every whole group of eleven advances the first coordinate by 0x10 and bumps a row counter, and each leftover column advances the second coordinate by 0x10 [code] (the two seed cells remain open [guess]). In effect an alien's screen coordinate is the fleet's reference corner plus sixteen pixels per grid step in each direction. Because loadReferenceAlienState refreshes loc_2009/loc_200a from the saved record on every pass, nudging that one reference corner slides the whole fleet in lockstep — that is the march.

The march is animated one alien at a time. A selector (loc_0141) refuses to pick a new alien while a draw is still outstanding — it returns early whenever ALIEN_DRAW_PENDING (0x2000) [seen] is set — and otherwise walks an index cursor forward through the 55-cell grid, wrapping back to the start when it runs off the end, until it lands on a live cell. It resolves that cell through alienIndexToScreenCoords, stows the resulting coordinate pair into ALIEN_DRAW_ADDR (0x200b) [seen] (overwriting the reference word that loadReferenceAlienState had parked there), and raises ALIEN_DRAW_PENDING to hand the work off. If the resolved coordinate has descended past a limit — it range-checks the value against 0x28 — the selector diverts elsewhere instead of drawing [guess]. The drawing pass (loc_0100) then reads ALIEN_DRAW_ADDR, blits the alien sixteen rows tall, and clears ALIEN_DRAW_PENDING, freeing the selector to advance to the next alien on the following pass. Advancing exactly one alien per pass is what makes the fleet ripple rather than jump.

Each alien also has a two-frame walk cycle, and selectAlternateSpriteFrame [code] is the toggle: it advances the sprite pointer by 0x30 (DE += 0x30) to the alternate frame's bank, and the drawing pass invokes it only when the alien's descriptor carries the alternate-frame flag [code], so aliens flip between their two poses as they step.

The horizontal sweep and the drop at the edges ride on FLEET_MOVE_DIR (0x200d) [seen]. The edge handler (loc_1597) reads that flag to choose which screen boundary to test; when the leading edge of the fleet reaches the boundary it flips FLEET_MOVE_DIR and stamps a sentinel into the working count at loc_2008 (0xfe when reversing one way, the row-drop distance the other) — the 0xfe case being the same sentinel loadReferenceAlienState watches for — so the reversal and the row-drop are carried in the very per-player state the reference loader reloads. FLEET_MOVE_DIR set means the fleet is sweeping one way; clear, the other.

Finally, the same reference corner supports the inverse mapping — from a screen coordinate back to a fleet-grid block — used when the game must know which grid cell a given point falls in. scaleXToBlock [code] steps the reference base held in loc_2009 up by 0x10 at a time until it reaches the coordinate in L (the coordinate is the threshold), returning the step count less one as a block index in B and the leftover as a residual in L/A; scaleYToBlock [code] does the same for loc_200a stepped up to a coordinate in H, leaving the residual in H/A. Both lean on the very cells that place the aliens (loc_2009/loc_200a, still open [guess]), so screen-to-grid and grid-to-screen share a single origin. Whether that origin's two bytes are the horizontal-versus-vertical pair — and hence whether the "X" and "Y" labels on these two routines are literal — is not yet pinned down.

## Player records, shields, shot, and prize

This subsystem is organized around the idea of an *active player*. A single work-RAM byte, the active-player page at `0x2067` (`ACTIVE_PLAYER_PAGE`, [code]), names both the RAM page that holds the current player's state and, in its low bit, which of the two players is currently on the machine. Almost every routine here begins by consulting that byte.

### Locating the active player's data

Three tiny helpers turn the page byte into a pointer. `activePlayerPageBase` (0x1611, [code]) simply shifts the page byte left eight bits, handing back `HL = mem[0x2067] << 8` — the base of the current player's page. `activeFieldRecordPointer` (0x0886, [code]) does the same but forces the low byte to `0xfc`, yielding `(page << 8) | 0xfc`, which addresses the field-save record near the top of that page. `readActivePlayerPageTopByte` (0x092e, [code]) builds on `activePlayerPageBase`, forces the low byte to `0xff`, and reads the byte sitting at the very top of the page into `A` (leaving that address in `HL`). Because the two player pages are `0x21xx` and `0x22xx`, these three produce `0x2100/0x2200`-relative pointers that follow whichever player is active.

`currentPlayerRecordPtr` (0x09ca, [code]) takes a different route to the *same* distinction: it rotates bit 0 of the page byte into carry and returns one of two fixed descriptor addresses — `PLAYER1_OBJ_DESC` at `0x20f8` ([code]) when the bit is set, `PLAYER2_OBJ_DESC` at `0x20fc` ([code]) when it is clear. So bit 0 of `0x2067` is the player selector: set means player 1 (page `0x21xx`, descriptor `0x20f8`), clear means player 2 (page `0x22xx`, descriptor `0x20fc`). Input follows the same selector: `readActivePlayerInput` (0x17c0, [code]) rotates bit 0 into carry and reads hardware input port 1 for player 1 (bit set) or port 2 for player 2 (bit clear), returning the byte in `A`.

`stageActivePlayerFieldSave` (0x0878, [code]) prepares to write the active player's field back to its record: it loads a count byte from `0x2008` into `B`, loads the reference-alien coordinate word from `0x2009` into `DE`, and then calls `activeFieldRecordPointer` so `HL` addresses the `page:0xfc` save slot. It is reached during the player-switch path, where the outgoing player's field is stashed before the incoming player's is restored.

### Generic record helpers

`advanceRecordTotals` (0x01d9, [seen]) is a small running-total accumulator over a four-byte record pointed at by `HL`: it reads the delta byte at `[HL+1]`, adds `C` into the total at `[HL+2]`, adds that delta into the second total at `[HL+3]`, and returns the second total in `A`. It is a shared primitive used by the scripted-animation stepper and other record-walking code, not tied to any one object.

A pair of eleven-byte scratch movers back the object-processing code. `copyRecordToWorkBuffer` (0x0550, [seen]) first stashes `A` into the scratch cell `0x207f`, then copies eleven bytes from the caller's source (`DE`) into the shared eleven-byte work buffer at `0x2073`. Its twin `copyWorkBufferToRecord` (0x055b, [code]) copies those same eleven bytes from `0x2073` back out to the caller's destination (`HL`). Together they lift an object's move-record into a fixed scratch strip for in-place work and write it back afterward.

### Sprite descriptors

`loadSpriteDescriptor` (0x1a3b, [code]) is the shared five-byte descriptor decoder. Reading forward from `HL`, it takes a two-byte graphics pointer into `DE`, then a coordinate byte into `A`, then two more bytes into `C` and `B`, and finally repoints `HL` at the composite address `C:A`. Several routines wrap it around a fixed record cell. `loadPlayerShotDescriptor` (0x0430, [code]) points at the player-shot descriptor `PLAYER_SHOT_DESC` at `0x2027` ([code]) and decodes it, leaving `HL` at the shot's screen coordinate word. `resolveSpriteScreenAddr` (0x0742, [code]) decodes the descriptor at `0x2087` and then folds the resulting pointer through the coordinate-to-screen conversion, so it returns with `HL` = screen address and `DE` = the graphics pointer. `loadDrawSequenceBlock` (0x0ae2, [code]) is a related loader that copies a twelve-byte draw/animation sequence from the caller's source (`DE`) into the fixed work slot at `0x20c2`.

### Shields

Each player owns a backup buffer for the four bunker shields: `PLAYER1_SHIELD_BUFFER` at `0x2142` ([code]) and `PLAYER2_SHIELD_BUFFER` at `0x2242` ([code]), both living inside that player's own `0x21xx`/`0x22xx` page. `initShieldBuffers` (0x01f8, [code]) fills such a buffer by replicating a `0x2c`-byte shield template (from `0x1d20`) four times into consecutive slots, `0x2c` bytes each, and hands back the end pointer. `initPlayer1ShieldBuffers` (0x01ef, [code]) and `initPlayer2ShieldBuffers` (0x01f5, [code]) are the thin front doors that seat the respective buffer base first; they are run when a player's round is being set up.

The screen-side work runs through `drawOrSaveShields` (0x021e, [seen]), the common four-block body. It first records the caller's `A` into the mode flag `SHIELD_SAVE_RESTORE_MODE` at `0x2081` ([code]), then walks four blocks down video RAM starting at `SHIELD_VRAM_BASE` (`0x2806`, [code]), each block a `0x16`-column by two-byte screen rectangle (`0x2c` bytes, matching the template) and each next block `0x02e0` bytes further down. On every pass it re-reads the mode flag: when the flag is set it captures the screen rectangle into the buffer (a *save*), and when clear it OR-merges the buffer's bitmap back onto the screen (a *restore*). That direction is fixed by the four public entries: `savePlayer1Shields` (0x0209, [code]) and `savePlayer2Shields` (0x020e, [code]) force the flag to 1 (save), while `restorePlayer1Shields` (0x021a, [code]) and `restorePlayer2Shields` (0x0213, [code]) force it to 0 (restore). Each of those in turn goes through `saveOrRestorePlayer1Shields` (0x021b, [code]) or `saveOrRestorePlayer2Shields` (0x0214, [code]), whose only job is to seat `DE` at the correct player's buffer base before running the shared body. In practice these fire around the player switch and round setup — the outgoing player's shields are captured, the incoming player's are painted back — so each player's bunker damage persists across turns.

### The player shot and firing

`PLAYER_SHOT_STATUS` at `0x2025` ([code]) is the player-shot state cell. `advanceRoundState` (0x1618, [code]) is the gated pre-round step that arms it. It does nothing unless a start sentinel at `0x2015` reads `0xff`, the field object cells at `0x2010`/`0x2011` are both idle, and `PLAYER_SHOT_STATUS` is zero. Once past that gate its behavior forks on `GAME_IN_PROGRESS` at `0x20ef` ([seen]). In attract mode (`GAME_IN_PROGRESS` zero) it stamps `PLAYER_SHOT_STATUS` to 1 and advances a scripted marching pointer, `ATTRACT_DEMO_PTR` at `0x20ed` ([code]): it increments the sixteen-bit pointer and, when the low byte reaches `0x7e`, wraps it back to `0x74` (a small demo window), then reads the byte at that pointer into `0x201d`. During real play (`GAME_IN_PROGRESS` nonzero) it instead watches the fire button through `FIRE_BUTTON_LATCH` at `0x202d` ([code]): if the latch is set it waits for the fire bit (mask `0x10`) to release before clearing the latch, and if the latch is clear it waits for a fresh fire press, then arms the shot by setting both `PLAYER_SHOT_STATUS` and `FIRE_BUTTON_LATCH` to 1. The latch is what enforces one shot per distinct press.

### Alien shot rate and rendering

The rate at which aliens fire scales with how much of the fleet remains. `selectAlienShotRate` (0x170e, [seen]) reads a field-size key from the second byte of the active player's descriptor (`currentPlayerRecordPtr()+1`), then scans the four-entry threshold table `ALIEN_SHOT_RATE_THRESHOLDS` at `0x1cb8` ([seen]) for the first entry that is greater than or equal to the key. The parallel byte from `ALIEN_SHOT_RATE_TABLE` at `0x1aa1` ([seen]) is stored to `0x20cf`, where the per-frame shot stepper later reads it — so a smaller surviving fleet selects a faster shot cadence.

An alien shot is drawn and erased through the descriptor at `ALIEN_SHOT_SPRITE_PTR` (`0x2079`, [code]). `drawAlienShotWithCollision` (0x066c, [code]) decodes that five-byte descriptor and then OR-blits the sprite column into video RAM with collision detection (the overlap sets the collision flag). `eraseAlienShot` (0x0675, [code]) decodes the same descriptor and instead ANDs the sprite's bits back out of the screen, removing it.

### The pending score-add packet

Three adjacent cells form a small pending-score-add record: `SCORE_ADD_PENDING` at `0x20f1` ([code]), `SCORE_ADD_VALUE` at `0x20f2` ([code]), and `SCORE_ADD_VALUE_HI` at `0x20f3` ([code]). They are written together, gated on `GAME_IN_PROGRESS`: the looked-up point value goes into `SCORE_ADD_VALUE`, its high byte is cleared into `SCORE_ADD_VALUE_HI`, and the pending flag `SCORE_ADD_PENDING` is raised to 1, so a later pass can fold the queued value into the score. (Only the scoring cue writes this packet; no routine in this subsystem consumes it.)

### The prize object

A distinct on-screen bonus object — the *prize* — is tracked by `PRIZE_ACTIVE` at `0x2002` ([code]) and torn down through a short chain. `deactivatePrize` (0x154a, [code]) is the base: it clears `PRIZE_ACTIVE` and masks bit 3 out of the sound shadow, silencing the prize's tone. `retirePrize` (0x1545, [code]) sits just above it, first stamping the shot-status cell `PLAYER_SHOT_STATUS` (`0x2025`) with 4 and then running the deactivation. Two entries lead into that retirement. `markExitingAndRetire` (0x1579, [code]) is reached when the descending object crosses the far edge; it raises the exit flag at `0x2085` before retiring. `tickDespawnTimer` (0x1538, [code]) drives the timed disappearance: it decrements a countdown at `loc_2003` (still `loc_`, [guess]/open) and returns while it is still nonzero; on expiry it reloads the object's stored screen position from `loc_2064` (still `loc_`, [guess]/open) into `HL`, clears the object's sixteen-row sprite column, and then runs the same retirement tail. The exact roles of the two despawn cells at `loc_2003` and `loc_2064` remain open.

## Attract screen and status display

The screen's fixed furniture -- the score header across the top, the CREDIT label at the bottom, the Taito copyright line, and the player's remaining-lives readout -- is painted by a family of small routines that each seat a handful of constants and hand off to the general glyph-drawing machinery.

drawScoreHeader (0x191a) [code] is the plainest of the type. It seats a run of 0x1c (28) sprite ids sourced from SCORE_HEADER_TEXT (0x1ae4) [code], points the screen pointer at SCORE_HEADER_SCREEN_ADDR (0x241e) [code], and hands off to the sprite-list driver drawSpriteList [code]. That driver walks the id list one byte at a time, drawing each id as an 8x8 glyph through drawSprite8x8 and stepping the destination along by one glyph per id, so the header comes out as a 28-glyph line of preset text [code].

drawCreditLabel (0x193c) [code] is cut from the same cloth: 0x07 (7) sprite ids from CREDIT_LABEL_TEXT (0x1fa9) [code] to CREDIT_LABEL_SCREEN_ADDR (0x3501) [code], again through drawSpriteList -- a seven-glyph label [code]. Header and label are emitted together during the screen's redraw pass (loc_1956 first blanks video RAM, then calls drawScoreHeader, three sibling draws, and drawCreditLabel in sequence, and loc_1979 likewise finishes on drawCreditLabel), which is why they read as one static frame [guess].

drawTaitoCopyright (0x199a) [code] paints its nine-glyph line -- TAITO_COPYRIGHT_TEXT (0x0bf7) [code] to TAITO_COPYRIGHT_SCREEN_ADDR (0x2e1b) [code], count 0x09 -- but only from behind a two-stage input gate. It keys off INPUT_CODE_STAGE_FLAG (0x201e) [code], a one-shot latch. While that latch is clear, every call reads input port 1, masks off the bits in 0x76, and demands the exact value 0x72; a mismatch returns without drawing, while a match bumps the latch to 1 [code]. Once armed, the routine reads port 1 a second time, masks the same 0x76, and now demands 0x34; only when that second code is present does it fall through to drawSpriteList and lay down the copyright [code]. The copyright is therefore not ordinary furniture -- it surfaces only after the specific two-step port-1 combination is entered, and because the first stage is latched it stays satisfied once seen, leaving just the second code to gate each subsequent draw [code]. The gate is reached through loc_0bf1, which calls another routine and then hands straight into it [guess].

The bottom status line -- how many lives remain -- is split across two routines. drawReserveLifeIcons (0x19e6) [code] seats the icon-strip base RESERVE_SHIP_ICONS_SCREEN_ADDR (0x2701) [code] and, when it is entered with a nonzero count in A, paints that many reserve-ship icons: each icon is a 16-byte blit of RESERVE_SHIP_SPRITE (0x1c60) [code] laid down through drawSpriteColumn [seen] (0x10 bytes at a 0x20 stride), with the destination walking forward one icon slot per pass, and the loop repeating A times [code]. After the final icon -- or immediately, if it was entered with the count-zero (Z) condition and skipped drawing altogether -- it calls clearScreenRegion [code] to blank the strips beyond the icons down to the terminator row, so icons left over from a higher life count get wiped [code]. Its sole caller (loc_1a7f) fetches the count, bails when it is zero, and passes count minus one, so the icons shown are the reserves held back beyond the ship currently in play [guess].

drawLivesDigit (0x1a8b) [code] supplies the numeric half of the readout. It seats LIVES_DIGIT_SCREEN_ADDR (0x2501) [code], reduces A to its low nibble (a value 0 through 15), and hands to drawDigit [code], which maps the nibble to its hex-glyph id by adding 0x1a and plots it through drawSprite8x8 -- a single-digit lives count at a fixed screen slot [code]. loc_1a7f arrives here by falling through immediately after drawReserveLifeIcons, restoring the true life count first, so the digit and the reserve icons together form one coherent readout [guess]; other callers reach the routine on their own, including one that zeroes A first to force a "0" (loc_166d) [guess].

## Frame tasks, timers, boot, and scoring

At cold start the machine paints its own work RAM into existence from a fixed template. `seedWorkRamImage` (0x01e4) sets a copy length of 0xc0 bytes and drops straight into `initWorkRam` (0x01e6) [code], which aims the copy from `WORKRAM_INIT_IMAGE` at 0x1b00 [seen] into the base of work RAM, `ALIEN_DRAW_PENDING` at 0x2000 [seen], and hands the move itself to `blockCopy` — the plain byte-for-byte loop that walks source and destination forward together [seen]. Those 0xc0 (192) bytes cover 0x2000–0x20bf, so the whole object/sprite work area is stamped down in one pass, with `GAME_OBJECT_TABLE` at 0x2010 [code] sitting inside that seeded block. `initWorkRam` is also reachable on its own (from loc_18d4 [guess]) with a caller-chosen copy length [code], while the 0xc0-preset form is re-run later on player handoff and round resets — the player switch loc_09ef, for instance, runs it with the running player index at 0x2067 saved across the call so the seed does not disturb it [code].

The single byte that records whether a game is live is `GAME_ACTIVE` at 0x20e9 [seen]. `setGameActive` (0x19d1) and `clearGameActive` (0x19d7) [code] both funnel through one shared store, `loc_19d3`, which writes the accumulator into 0x20e9 [seen]; the former hands it 1, the latter 0. The flag is raised where a game or round begins (loc_0814/loc_0817, loc_16c9) and dropped on death, reset, and handoff (loc_1979, loc_028e, loc_16e6, the per-frame sound tick loc_17cd, loc_079b) [guess], and the player-switch path loc_09ef additionally zeroes 0x20e9 directly [code]. Everything downstream treats it as the master gate: both interrupt bodies load 0x20e9 first and, finding it zero, fall straight to their epilogue without touching the play field [code].

Those interrupt bodies are where per-frame work is scheduled. The vblank-time routine loc_0010 [guess] decrements a frame counter at 0x20c0, runs the sound tick, samples the coin/input port, and keeps a BCD counter at 0x20eb by adding one and decimal-adjusting it on eligible frames — the only score-style arithmetic in this group [code] — before dispatching on `GAME_ACTIVE`. The mid-screen routine loc_008c [guess] clears a busy byte at 0x2072, checks `GAME_ACTIVE`, and then, unless a separate flag at 0x20ef forces the draw, consults the per-frame task bitfield `TASK_FLAGS` at 0x20c1 [code]: it rotates the low bit out and runs its draw pair only when that bit is set [code], so 0x20c1 is the one-byte record of which drawing task the frame owes. `loc_1982` is that cell's writer, storing the accumulator into 0x20c1 [seen].

Timing that is not tied to the raster runs off countdown cells. `TIMER_RELOAD` at 0x0600 [code] is the reload constant for the flying-saucer cadence: `loc_0913` [code] counts a rolling 16-bit timer down on each pass while its gate cell sits below 0x78, and when that count reaches zero it reloads from `TIMER_RELOAD` and raises a wrap flag before storing the decremented value, giving the saucer a fixed, free-running interval [code]. A second mode byte, `TWO_PLAYER_GAME` at 0x20ce [code], guards mode-specific screen work — `blankScreenStrip` [code] checks it and skips its strip-clear when the cell is already set.

Two small helpers feed setup and placement. `readStartingShips` (0x08d1) [code] reads input port 2, keeps the low two bits, and adds 3, yielding a 3..6 starting-lives selector taken straight off the ships dip switch; it is pulled in at game and round setup (loc_079b, loc_0aea) [guess]. `countStepsToThreshold` (0x1554) [code] is the coordinate-scaling primitive: it counts, in C, how many 0x10 steps lift A up to or past a threshold in H, first normalizing a negative A upward through `loc_1590` [code], and exits with carry deliberately cleared for the callers that read it. `scaleXToBlock` and `scaleYToBlock` [code] drive it with an object's X or Y coordinate as the threshold to turn a pixel coordinate into a coarse block index plus the leftover residual — the mapping from screen position onto the coarse grid the object logic works in.

## What is not described yet

The control-flow spine — the main frame loop, the object-state dispatcher and its per-state handlers, the two RST
interrupt handlers, collision, and the attract-mode demo — is not yet recovered and is not described here. A few
helper routines also keep `loc_<addr>` names because their role is not yet confident from the code alone:
`loc_0a5f` (queues a points value), `loc_172c` (gates a sound bit on the shot status), `loc_073c` (draws a
descriptor sprite), and `loc_00d7` (a per-player init seeding `loc_21fb`/`loc_22fb`). The reference-alien anchor
pair `loc_2009`/`loc_200a`, the saucer sprite pointer `loc_2087` and active flag `loc_2083`, the alien-shot rate
cell `loc_20cf`, the object work buffer `loc_2073`, the disputed despawn cells `loc_2003`/`loc_2064`, and several
other work-RAM and ROM cells keep `loc_` names pending grounding. These resolve at grounding or when their callers
lift and their roles become confident from the code.

Additional leaf helper routines — and the work-RAM and ROM cells they touch — are present in the idiomatic
layer under `loc_<addr>` names, their roles pending a naming pass and so not narrated here until they are named.
